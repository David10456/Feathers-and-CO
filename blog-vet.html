Veterinary Care and Biosecurity: Protecting Our Flock Through Prevention and Precision

  • Feathers and CO Team
  • Aug 8, 2026
  • 3 Comments

At Feathers and CO POULTRY FARM, we operate under a simple but powerful philosophy: prevention is infinitely better than cure. In poultry farming, where diseases can spread rapidly through an entire flock within hours, biosecurity and proactive veterinary care are not optional—they are the foundation of our entire operation. Our comprehensive health management system in Nkolafamba combines strict biosecurity protocols, strategic vaccination programs, rapid disease detection, and strong veterinary partnerships to ensure our 1,000 broilers, 20 agric-fowls, and 30 old layers remain healthy, productive, and safe for our customers.

Every decision we make about bird health is guided by science, data, and a deep commitment to animal welfare. We do not wait for problems to appear—we actively prevent them through multiple layers of protection, monitoring, and rapid response capabilities.

"A healthy flock is a productive flock. But more importantly, a healthy flock means safe food for our customers and peace of mind for their families. That responsibility drives everything we do."

— Feathers and CO Veterinary Consultant

Our Biosecurity Framework: Multiple Layers of Protection

Biosecurity is the first line of defense against disease introduction and spread. Our facility implements a multi-layered biosecurity system:

  • Physical Barriers: The entire farm is enclosed by a 1.50-meter-high perimeter fence, preventing unauthorized human and animal entry. Each poultry house has its own secondary barrier with controlled access points.
  • Access Control: Only authorized personnel can enter the farm. All visitors must sign a biosecurity log, wear provided protective clothing (coveralls, boots, hairnets), and pass through a footbath containing disinfectant solution before entering any poultry house.
  • Vehicle Disinfection: All vehicles entering the farm premises must pass through a wheel dip containing a broad-spectrum disinfectant. Feed delivery trucks are sprayed down before unloading.
  • Pest and Wild Bird Control: We maintain strict pest control protocols to prevent rodents, insects, and wild birds from entering poultry houses, as these are common disease vectors. Netting covers all ventilation openings, and we conduct regular pest inspections.
  • Equipment Dedication: Each poultry house has its own dedicated equipment (feeders, waterers, cleaning tools). No equipment is shared between houses to prevent cross-contamination.
  • All-In, All-Out System: We practice strict all-in, all-out management, meaning all birds in a house are the same age and are moved out together. This allows for complete cleaning and disinfection between batches, breaking disease cycles.

Quarantine Protocols: Protecting Against New Introductions

Any new birds introduced to the farm undergo a mandatory quarantine period:

  • Isolation Period: All day-old chicks and purchased birds are housed in a separate quarantine facility for a minimum of 14 days before being introduced to the main flock.
  • Health Observation: During quarantine, birds are closely monitored for any signs of illness, respiratory issues, or abnormal behavior.
  • Veterinary Examination: A veterinarian conducts a thorough health check of all quarantined birds before they are cleared to join the main flock.
  • Vaccination Verification: We verify that all incoming birds have received appropriate vaccinations from the hatchery, and we administer any booster vaccines as needed during the quarantine period.
  • Separate Staff: Staff working in the quarantine area do not work in the main poultry houses on the same day, preventing potential disease transmission.

Vaccination Program: Strategic Disease Prevention

Our vaccination program is designed in consultation with our veterinary partners and follows national guidelines while being tailored to our specific location and risk factors:

Broiler Vaccination Schedule:

  • Day 1 (at Hatchery): Marek's disease vaccine administered subcutaneously.
  • Day 7: Newcastle disease vaccine (B1 strain) via eye drop or drinking water.
  • Day 14: Infectious bursal disease (Gumboro) vaccine via drinking water.
  • Day 21: Newcastle disease booster (LaSota strain) via drinking water.
  • Day 28: Infectious bursal disease booster via drinking water (if needed based on local prevalence).

Layer Vaccination Schedule (Extended Program):

  • Day 1-21: Same as broilers for initial protection.
  • Week 6-8: Fowl pox vaccine via wing web stab.
  • Week 10-12: Infectious bronchitis vaccine via drinking water.
  • Week 16-18: Newcastle disease (killed vaccine) via injection for long-term immunity during laying period.
  • Ongoing: Regular booster vaccinations as recommended by our veterinarian based on local disease surveillance data.

All vaccinations are administered by trained staff under veterinary supervision. We maintain detailed vaccination records for every batch, which are accessible through our QR code traceability system.

Disinfection Protocols: Eliminating Pathogens

Regular and thorough disinfection is critical for maintaining a disease-free environment:

  • Footbaths: Placed at every entrance to poultry houses, containing fresh disinfectant solution (changed daily). Common disinfectants include glutaraldehyde-based solutions and quaternary ammonium compounds.
  • House Disinfection (Between Batches): After each batch is removed, houses undergo a comprehensive 7-step cleaning and disinfection process:
    1. Dry cleaning: Remove all litter, feed residues, and debris.
    2. High-pressure washing: Clean all surfaces with water and detergent.
    3. Drying period: Allow 24-48 hours for complete drying.
    4. Disinfection application: Apply broad-spectrum disinfectant (formalin-based or hydrogen peroxide-based) to all surfaces using foam sprayers for maximum contact time.
    5. Fumigation: Seal the house and fumigate with formaldehyde or hydrogen peroxide vapor for 24 hours.
    6. Resting period: Leave the house empty for a minimum of 14 days to break pathogen life cycles.
    7. Final inspection: Veterinarian conducts a swab test to verify disinfection efficacy before new birds are introduced.
  • Water Line Disinfection: Water lines are flushed and treated with chlorine dioxide or hydrogen peroxide between batches to remove biofilm and prevent bacterial growth.
  • Equipment Disinfection: All feeders, waterers, and tools are soaked in disinfectant solution, scrubbed, rinsed, and dried before being returned to service.

Disease Monitoring and Early Detection

Early detection is crucial for preventing disease outbreaks. Our monitoring systems include:

  • Daily Health Checks: Staff conduct visual inspections of all birds twice daily, looking for signs of illness: lethargy, respiratory distress (sneezing, coughing, nasal discharge), diarrhea, abnormal posture, or reduced feed/water intake.
  • Mortality Tracking: Daily mortality is recorded and analyzed. Any sudden increase triggers immediate veterinary investigation.
  • IoT Environmental Monitoring: Our sensor network detects changes in temperature, humidity, and ammonia levels that could indicate respiratory issues or environmental stress that predisposes birds to disease.
  • Water Consumption Monitoring: A sudden drop in water intake is often the earliest indicator of disease, often appearing 24-48 hours before visible clinical signs. Our digital water meters alert us immediately to abnormal patterns.
  • Feed Intake Monitoring: Load cells on feed silos track consumption. Reduced feed intake is another early warning sign.
  • Post-Mortem Examination: Any bird that dies is examined by our veterinary consultant to determine the cause of death and identify potential disease threats to the flock.

Common Poultry Diseases and Our Prevention Strategies

While we work hard to prevent all diseases, we are prepared to manage common threats:

  • Newcastle Disease: A highly contagious viral disease. Prevention: Strict vaccination program, biosecurity, and avoiding contact with wild birds.
  • Infectious Bursal Disease (Gumboro): Viral disease affecting the immune system. Prevention: Vaccination at correct timing, thorough disinfection between batches.
  • Coccidiosis: Parasitic disease causing intestinal damage. Prevention: Use of coccidiostats in feed, maintaining dry litter conditions, avoiding overcrowding.
  • Respiratory Diseases (CRD, Infectious Bronchitis): Prevention: Proper ventilation to reduce ammonia levels, vaccination, avoiding sudden temperature changes, minimizing dust.
  • Fowl Pox: Viral disease causing skin lesions. Prevention: Wing web vaccination, mosquito control (vectors).
  • Salmonellosis: Bacterial disease with food safety implications. Prevention: Rodent control, water disinfection, proper feed storage, strict hygiene during processing.

Treatment Protocols: When Intervention is Necessary

When disease does occur, we follow strict treatment protocols:

  • Veterinary Diagnosis: Our consulting veterinarian conducts a thorough examination, often including laboratory tests (blood samples, swabs, post-mortem) to identify the specific pathogen.
  • Targeted Treatment: Based on diagnosis, appropriate treatment is administered. We prefer targeted treatments over broad-spectrum approaches to minimize antibiotic use.
  • Antibiotic Stewardship: We use antibiotics ONLY when absolutely necessary and under veterinary prescription. We NEVER use antibiotics for growth promotion or routine prevention. When antibiotics are required, we strictly observe withdrawal periods before processing to ensure no residues remain in the meat.
  • Isolation of Sick Birds: Any bird showing signs of illness is immediately removed from the flock and placed in an isolation facility for treatment and observation.
  • Enhanced Biosecurity: During a disease outbreak, we implement enhanced biosecurity measures: restricted movement, increased disinfection frequency, and separation of staff working with sick birds from those working with healthy birds.
  • Documentation: All treatments are documented in detail, including the medication used, dosage, duration, and withdrawal period. This information is recorded in our digital system and accessible via our QR code traceability.

Veterinary Partnerships and Professional Support

We do not operate alone. Our veterinary health program is supported by professional partnerships:

  • Consulting Veterinarian: We have a retainer agreement with a qualified poultry veterinarian who conducts weekly farm visits, reviews health data, adjusts vaccination programs, and responds immediately to any health concerns.
  • Laboratory Services: We partner with accredited veterinary diagnostic laboratories for rapid testing of samples (blood, tissue, swabs) to identify pathogens and guide treatment decisions.
  • Government Veterinary Services: We maintain close communication with the Ministry of Livestock, Fisheries, and Animal Industries, reporting any notifiable diseases and staying updated on regional disease surveillance information.
  • Continuing Education: Our staff receives regular training on biosecurity, disease recognition, and proper medication administration from our veterinary partners.

Mortality Management and Disposal

Proper disposal of dead birds is critical for preventing disease spread:

  • Daily Collection: Dead birds are collected from houses at least twice daily.
  • Incineration: We use a dedicated incinerator to safely dispose of all mortalities at temperatures that completely destroy pathogens.
  • Record Keeping: Every mortality is recorded with date, house, and cause of death (if determined). This data is analyzed to identify trends and potential problems.
  • Never Sold or Reused: Dead birds are NEVER sold, processed, or fed to other animals. They are completely destroyed through incineration.

Results: The Impact of Our Veterinary Program

Our comprehensive veterinary care and biosecurity program delivers measurable results:

  • Low Mortality Rates: We consistently maintain mortality rates below 3% for broilers and below 5% for layers, significantly better than the industry average of 8-12% in Cameroon.
  • Minimal Antibiotic Use: Our preventive approach means we use antibiotics less than 5% of the time, compared to industry averages of 20-30%.
  • Consistent Performance: Our birds achieve target weights and egg production rates consistently, batch after batch.
  • Food Safety Assurance: We have never had a food safety incident or tested positive for antibiotic residues in our products.
  • Customer Confidence: Our transparent approach to animal health, documented through our QR code system, has built unshakeable trust with our customers.

At Feathers and CO, veterinary care is not just about treating sick birds—it is about creating an environment where birds cannot get sick in the first place. Through rigorous biosecurity, strategic vaccination, rapid detection, and professional veterinary support, we ensure that every chicken and every egg we produce is safe, healthy, and of the highest quality. This is our promise to our customers, and it is a promise we keep every single day.

I am a final-year veterinary student at the University of Dschang, and I am writing my thesis on antimicrobial resistance in Cameroonian poultry farms. Your approach to antibiotic stewardship is impressive. Could you share more details about how you decide when to use antibiotics versus when to rely on supportive care? Also, do you perform any antibiotic sensitivity testing to guide your treatment choices?

Feathers and CO Team Reply
Aug 11, 2026

Hello Jean-Marc! That is an excellent and highly relevant thesis topic—antimicrobial resistance is one of the biggest challenges facing modern medicine and agriculture. Our decision-making process for antibiotic use follows a strict hierarchy: 1) First, we optimize environmental conditions (temperature, ventilation, litter quality) and nutrition to support the bird's natural immune system; 2) For mild cases, we use supportive care: electrolytes, vitamins, probiotics, and isolation; 3) Antibiotics are used ONLY when: a) The disease is confirmed bacterial (not viral), b) The bird's suffering or mortality risk is significant, c) Supportive care alone is insufficient. When we do use antibiotics, yes, we absolutely perform antibiotic sensitivity testing (antibiograms) through our partner laboratory whenever possible. This allows us to choose the most effective, narrow-spectrum antibiotic rather than using broad-spectrum drugs empirically. We would be honored to support your thesis research—please contact us through our website, and we can arrange a farm visit and share anonymized data on our antibiotic usage patterns and sensitivity testing results. Best of luck with your thesis!
